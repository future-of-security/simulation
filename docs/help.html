Help & Rules

Everything you need to know to play the simulation

How the Simulation Works

You are part of a crisis management exercise. Each team plays a specific role in responding to a cascading crisis scenario set in Southwest Virginia.

  • Teams: 12, of three or four students each
  • Duration: 60 minutes per phase
  • Roles rotate each phase — your team gets a new role and starting conditions

Each Phase

  1. Check the dashboard for your team's role, budget, and trust level
  2. Review the active incidents visible to your role
  3. Coordinate with other teams if needed
  4. Submit your response report on Canvas
  5. The facilitator processes responses and updates the dashboard with new incidents and scores

Steps 2 through 5 repeat several times inside the class hour. Submit as often as you want; those in-class submissions are working drafts and none of them is your last.

Two Stages, One Deadline

The phase itself runs in class and ends when the facilitator closes it and starts the debrief. The assignment stays open until 11:59pm that night. Everything you submit during the phase is read by the simulation and moves the board; your rubric grade is read from the last report you file, which is why it has to carry the two sections that look back on the whole phase — the ones you could not have written mid-class.

You never have to guess which submission is your final one: nothing you submit during class can be, because the deadline is hours after class ends. Two sections of the report exist only in that final version, since they look back on the phase as a whole.

The deadline is hard. Canvas locks at the same moment it comes due, so there is no submission after it and no late credit.

Filling In the Report

The template carries headings and formats only. What each section is for, and what a filled-in line looks like, is here — so that nothing you paste into Canvas is something we asked you to delete first.

Team / Role / Phase

Your team's codename, the role on your dashboard card, and the phase number. The team name is how your report is matched to your board, so write it exactly as the dashboard spells it.

What My Role Could See

List the incidents visible to your role, by the titles the dashboard uses. Then list what you needed to know and could not see — the gap is part of the exercise, and naming it earns more than pretending it was not there.

Incidents visible to my role:
- Hospital Electronic Records Inaccessible

What I needed to know but could not see:
- whether the outage reached the rural clinics — no visibility from this role

Situation Judgment

Two or three sentences: what you believe is happening, and how confident you are. A guess you name as a guess is worth more than a certainty you cannot support.

Actions Taken

One line per action, in the format the template shows. The Action ID has to come from your phase's catalog; the last field is why the action is yours to take.

- A14 | Deploy backup generators | $1.2M | we own the distribution assets

Collaboration and Transfers

One line per team you worked with. Leave confirmed as no if you have not heard back. Both teams file their own report, so a transfer only one side records is a dispute, not a mistake — and a cost split the other team never records is charged to you in full.

- Glacier | Coordinated triage capacity | $2.0M from us to Glacier | confirmed: yes

Proposed Custom Actions

Optional. Something not in the catalog that your team wants to attempt; the facilitator rules on whether it is feasible and what it costs. The justification is the part that decides it: what resources or authority does your role actually hold that makes this reasonable?

- Stand up a joint call center with Shentel | $0.4M | cuts duplicate 911 traffic | 2 hours | may collapse under load

Justification: we run the regional dispatch centre and already hold the trunk contract.

Disputes

Optional, and file it as soon as you notice. Anything from an earlier update cycle that seems unreasonable: how an action was evaluated, scoring, a trust change, an inject outcome, a custom action ruling.

- 9:20 update | our A3 was charged in full | we recorded a 50/50 split with Yellowstone

Remaining Budget

What is left in your budget column after the actions above. The board is the record; this is a check that you and it agree.

Outcomes, Needs, Risks — final report only

What you expect to happen next, what you still need and from whom, and the risks you are watching. This looks back on the phase as a whole, so only the last report you file before the deadline that night can carry it.

Reflection and Design Takeaway — final report only

One mechanic in this phase that forced a real decision or fell flat, what you argued about it, and what you would change if this were your simulation.

- the budget cap did nothing — we never came close to spending it
- we argued that a transfer should cost both teams an action, not just an agreement
- we would make trust decay when a team goes a whole phase without communicating

This is the record you draw on for the Integration Lab and for the simulation design section of your manual — write it now, not from memory in October. It is also what the reflection criterion is scored from, so a blank here is a blank score whatever you said in the room.

How the Report Is Graded

Each phase is worth 100 points, scored against the rubric attached to that phase's assignment on Canvas. Open the assignment and click Show Rubric: it lists all five criteria, what section of your report each is read from, and what separates a strong answer from a thin one. That rubric is the only copy, so it is the one to check.

Which submission is graded

Your rubric grade comes from one report: the last one you file. That is the report carrying the two sections only it can carry — Outcomes, Needs, Risks and Reflection and Design Takeaway — and it is the only one a grader opens.

This is not the same as your work being ignored. Every report you file during the phase is read by the simulation as it arrives, and what it says your team did is what moves the board: your budget, your trust, the state of the incidents you worked, and which branch of the phase opens next. That is a separate reading with separate consequences, and it happens while you are still playing.

Who reads what

The rubric is split between two readers.

  • Three criteria are scored by an AI served from llm.arc.vt.edu, read from what you write during the phase: decision quality under uncertainty, engagement with the assigned role, and coordination with other teams.
  • Two are scored by a member of the teaching team, read from the sections you write after the debrief: crisis communication, from Outcomes, Needs, Risks, and reflection and design takeaway. Those two are 40 of the 100 points.

Every criterion comes back with a comment saying what earned the number. If you think one is wrong, the comment is the thing to argue with.

What does not earn points

Volume earns nothing. A long report that never names its own uncertainty scores below a short one that does, and reporting on incidents your role cannot see is not rewarded. An empty section earns nothing either, whether you leave it blank or fill it with filler — so the shortest report that shows your team acted is the best one.

Your score on the dashboard is not your grade, and a hard phase played badly can still make an excellent report. What the rubric asks is whether you can account for what you did and what it cost.

Actions & Budget

Using Catalog Actions

The Action Catalog lists all pre-approved actions your team can take. Each action has:

  • Available To: Which roles can use this action
  • Cost: Deducted from your budget
  • Delay: Time before the action takes effect (0 = immediate)
  • Requires Approval: Some actions need another team's sign-off
  • Trust Impact: How this action affects your public trust score

Custom Actions

You can propose actions not in the catalog. The facilitator will evaluate feasibility, adjust costs, and determine outcomes. Custom actions can be:

  • Approved — proceeds as proposed
  • Approved with modifications — cost or effect adjusted
  • Partially effective — helps but less than hoped
  • Denied — not feasible for your team
  • Backfires — makes things worse

Cost Splitting on Collaborative Actions

When multiple teams collaborate on the same catalog action targeting the same incident, they split the cost equally:

CollaborationCost per Team
2 teams50% each
3 teams33% each
4+ teamsEqual split

Requirements: the split applies only when both teams document the collaboration — see Collaboration.

No split applies when:

  • Teams take the same action independently on different incidents — each pays the full cost
  • Teams take different actions on the same incident — each pays their own action's full cost
  • Custom actions — costs are evaluated per proposal, not split

Allocation

Your budget is deliberately smaller than your own action catalog. Each phase you are allocated roughly 0.6× the cost of every catalog action available to your role, plus a flat $25K for custom actions. Acting alone, you cannot buy everything your role could do — you either give something up, split the cost, or ask for a transfer.

The allocation is all you get, and it does not carry. Every phase is its own economy: what you have not spent when the phase ends is gone, and nothing rewards hoarding it — points come from what your actions did. Because roles rotate every phase, the allocation rotates with them — over six phases every team holds both expensive and cheap roles, and each phase's budget is sized to the role you hold that phase.

Trust is the thing that carries. Money resets between phases; your reputation does not. See Trust System.

Budget Transfers

Teams can transfer budget to other teams to fund actions they can't take themselves. Both teams must document the transfer. The receiving team must still take the action — a transfer alone doesn't resolve anything.

Trust System

Trust represents public confidence in your organization. It starts at the value shown on the dashboard and changes based on your actions. Unlike your budget, trust carries between phases: what you end one phase with is what you start the next one with, whatever role you are holding by then. A reputation belongs to the team that earned it, not to the office it was sitting in.

TrustEffect
8–10Actions cost 20% less, community cooperation, faster approvals
5–7Normal effectiveness
3–4Actions cost 25% more, community resistance, media scrutiny
1–2Actions may fail (25% chance), protests, delayed responses

What Changes Trust

  • +1 to +2: Successful actions, good communication, collaboration, creative solutions
  • −1 to −2: Failed or misleading communication, ignoring critical incidents, backfired actions
  • −1: Not submitting a response when you had severity 4–5 incidents
  • Trust is a whole number from 0 to 10 — the same number you see on the dashboard, not a percentage.
  • One report moves it by at most ±2, and a whole update cycle by at most ±3, however many things you did in it.

Scoring

Teams earn points by resolving incidents. There are no penalty points — you earn for successes, not punished for failures.

Resolution Points

Every incident carries its own point value. What your team earns is a fixed share of it, decided by one of five words for what your actions did:

OutcomeShare of the incident's points
Fully resolved — the incident is done100%
Strong partial — directly addressed, most of it solved50%
Moderate partial — helped, root cause still standing30%
Indirect — a side effect of something else you did15%
None — touched the incident, changed nothing0%

There is nothing between those five. A 100-point incident pays 100, 50, 30, 15 or 0, and which one is decided by what your report shows you did against that incident's own standard for being closed.

Every Team on an Incident Is Scored Separately

Points are not divided between the teams working an incident. If three of you work the same incident and all three reports show work that closed it, all three earn the full share — your score is what your team did, judged on its own.

So collaboration never costs you points. What it saves you is money: a shared action is split, and both sides gain +1 trust for documenting it. That is the whole reward, and it is why an action your budget cannot cover alone is still worth taking with a partner.

Deadlines and Escalation

Each incident has a time limit. An incident nobody has acted on when its limit passes escalates on the clock, and what opens next is the branch that follows from it having escalated rather than from it having been closed.

Acting on an incident after it escalates still earns what your outcome is worth — the points are for what you did, not for beating the clock. What you cannot get back is the branch: the phase has already moved on to the world where nobody answered in time.

Actions That Take Time

An action with a delay in the catalog produces its effect that many minutes after you take it, and an effect that has not landed is not a resolution. If your report says what the action produced, it is scored on that. If it only says the action was taken, it is scored as started work — so when a check comes back, file what it found.

Collaboration

Many incidents can only be resolved through cross-team action, and the rules reward it two ways: the cost of a shared action is split, and both teams gain trust. Your points are not divided — each team is scored on what it did — so working together costs you nothing in score and buys you an action your own budget could not cover.

  • Both teams must document it in their own response reports. A collaboration only one side records earns neither of you the cost split nor the trust — it is a dispute, and the team that claimed it pays the full price.
  • Collaborating teams get +1 trust each, once the other side's report confirms it.

Intelligence Sharing

Each phase's action catalog includes an Intelligence Sharing action available to ALL teams at no cost. Sharing information with other teams builds trust and improves collective response. Check your phase's action catalog for the specific action ID.

Tips for Success

  • Read your incidents carefully — click each one on the dashboard for full details
  • Check which actions you can take — filter the action catalog by your role
  • Watch your budget — you can't spend more than you have, and you were not given enough to do everything; decide what to drop before the clock does it for you
  • Coordinate early — reach out to teams whose roles complement yours
  • Propose custom actions — creative solutions are welcome and earn trust
  • Don't ignore critical incidents — severity 4-5 incidents that go unaddressed will escalate and cost you trust
  • Submit promptly — not submitting costs trust and lets incidents escalate
  • Track your spending — list remaining budget in your report
